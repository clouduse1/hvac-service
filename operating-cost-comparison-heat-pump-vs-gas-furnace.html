The Real Numbers Behind Operating Cost Comparisons Between Heat Pumps and Gas Furnaces

Operating cost is often the deciding variable in the heat pump vs. gas furnace conversation, and it is also the variable most commonly misrepresented. Simple comparisons that pit electricity cost per kWh against natural gas cost per therm miss the crucial multiplier of equipment efficiency and produce misleading answers. The actual comparison requires walking through the delivered heat cost per BTU output for each system at each specific utility rate market, which is what this guide does.

The framework applies to any homeowner comparing the two systems on a specific address. It uses the underlying physics rather than assumptions, and it accounts for the seasonal variability that a single-point comparison misses. Readers should come away with the ability to run the numbers for their own utility rates rather than relying on national averages.

The Correct Way to Compare Cost per BTU Delivered

Both technologies deliver heat measured in BTU per hour. The correct cost comparison is dollars per useful BTU delivered to the house, not dollars per unit of fuel input. That means the calculation has to include equipment efficiency.

For a gas furnace: cost per delivered BTU equals gas rate per therm divided by (100,000 BTU per therm times AFUE). At $1.20 per therm and 95 percent AFUE: $1.20 divided by (100,000 times 0.95) equals $0.0000126 per BTU, or $12.60 per million BTU delivered.

For a heat pump: cost per delivered BTU equals electricity rate per kWh divided by (3,412 BTU per kWh times the seasonal COP). At $0.16 per kWh and a seasonal COP of 2.5 (which is a fair average for a mid-tier heat pump in a hot-humid climate): $0.16 divided by (3,412 times 2.5) equals $0.0000188 per BTU, or $18.80 per million BTU delivered. Wait, that shows the furnace winning at U.S. average rates for that COP.

Change one variable: a variable-speed heat pump with seasonal COP of 3.5 in the same rate market. $0.16 divided by (3,412 times 3.5) equals $0.0000134 per BTU, or $13.40 per million BTU delivered. Now the heat pump wins on operating cost. The COP variable is decisive.

What Seasonal COP Actually Looks Like

Seasonal COP is the average COP across all the operating hours in a heating season, weighted by the actual outdoor temperature distribution the equipment sees. It is not the same as the rated COP at 47 F test conditions or the HSPF2 rating divided by 3.412 (that shortcut over-estimates seasonal performance for most climates).

For a properly sized system in ASHRAE zone 2 or 3, seasonal COP typically falls in these ranges:

  • Single-stage standard-efficiency heat pump (HSPF2 around 7.5): seasonal COP roughly 2.4 to 2.7.
  • Two-stage mid-tier heat pump (HSPF2 around 8.5): seasonal COP roughly 2.8 to 3.2.
  • Variable-speed high-efficiency heat pump (HSPF2 around 9.5): seasonal COP roughly 3.3 to 3.8.
  • Cold-climate variable-speed heat pump (HSPF2 above 10): seasonal COP roughly 3.4 to 4.0.

Rate Markets Where Each System Wins

The rate-market question can be reduced to a simple crossover: at what gas-to-electricity rate ratio does the heat pump start winning on operating cost? At a fixed COP of 3.0 and AFUE of 95 percent, the heat pump wins when the gas rate per therm exceeds roughly 0.66 times the electricity rate per kWh multiplied by 10 (to normalize units). In plain English: at $0.16 electricity, the heat pump wins whenever gas is above roughly $1.05 per therm.

National average gas rates fluctuate but tend to sit near $1.15 to $1.30 per therm in residential markets, so heat pumps tend to win on operating cost across most of the U.S. at national average electricity rates. Regional exceptions exist in specific markets with very cheap gas or expensive electricity.

The Utility Bill Reality Check

A homeowner's actual utility bills for the past 12 to 24 months are the best data source for a specific address. The bills reveal the local rate market including any tiered pricing, fixed charges, delivery surcharges, and seasonal differentials that a national average cannot capture. The exercise: total the winter heating energy consumption in a fuel comparison, translate into total delivered heating BTU based on the current equipment, and compare against a modeled scenario with the alternative equipment.

Most homeowners overestimate the impact of the fuel input rate and underestimate the impact of equipment efficiency. A high-efficiency system running on the more expensive fuel often beats a low-efficiency system running on the cheaper fuel, which is counter to intuition but correct arithmetically.

Beyond the Utility Bill: Other Cost Dimensions

Operating cost is the largest ongoing cost dimension but not the only one. First cost, service and maintenance, and eventual replacement cost also factor into a full lifecycle comparison. On a total-cost-of-ownership basis for a 15-year horizon in a hot-humid climate, high-efficiency heat pumps typically come out ahead because operating savings compound each year against a first-cost premium that stays fixed. Federal and state incentives further tilt the picture in favor of heat pumps in most current rate markets.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do simple electricity-vs-gas cost comparisons produce misleading answers?

Because they compare fuel prices without accounting for the efficiency of how each fuel becomes heat. Natural gas at $1.20 per therm sounds cheap next to electricity at $0.16 per kWh, but a therm of gas at 95 percent AFUE delivers 95,000 BTU while a kWh of electricity at COP 3.5 delivers 11,942 BTU. Dividing rate by BTU output shows the real cost per delivered BTU, which is the number that matters for utility bill impact.

How does the homeowner find seasonal COP for a specific heat pump?

Manufacturers publish HSPF2 ratings that are useful comparison metrics but do not translate directly into seasonal COP. A more accurate estimate comes from the AHRI product listing that specifies rated capacity and power draw at 47 F and 17 F outdoor temperatures, combined with the local temperature distribution for the specific address. HVAC designers use software like WrightSoft or Manual J tools that model seasonal performance for the specific installation.

Do gas prices tend to rise or fall over time?

Historically gas prices have been volatile with sustained periods of both. Long-term forecasts published by the U.S. Energy Information Administration project modest real increases over the next decade in most scenarios. Homeowners committing to a 15 to 20 year equipment decision should account for rate volatility on both sides rather than assuming current rates will hold.

Are there incentives that lower the heat pump first cost?

Yes. The federal Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit currently provides up to $2,000 in tax credit for qualifying heat pumps installed in a primary residence. Many state and utility programs stack additional rebates on top, sometimes reaching several thousand dollars for high-efficiency equipment. Rebates for high-efficiency gas furnaces exist but are usually smaller. Program details change over time so homeowners should verify current terms before committing.

How much does maintenance cost per year for each system?

Annual maintenance runs $150 to $300 per year for either technology on a residential single-system installation. Furnaces have combustion equipment that needs cleaning and safety verification; heat pumps have refrigerant and both indoor and outdoor coils that need cleaning. The cost difference is minor and not usually decisive in the operating comparison.

Does a variable-speed system pay back its premium?

In most climates and rate markets, yes, on a 10 to 15 year horizon. Variable-speed heat pumps deliver higher seasonal COP through more partial-load runtime, and variable-speed furnaces deliver higher seasonal AFUE through longer low-fire runtimes. Both also improve comfort and dehumidification. In markets with very short heating seasons (deep South) the payback horizon on a variable-speed furnace stretches because the equipment sees fewer heating hours; a variable-speed heat pump still pays back reliably because the same equipment does the cooling too.

What is the total cost of ownership over 15 years?

A rough model: gas furnace plus AC pair with $12,000 first cost and $1,600 annual heating and cooling utility cost totals roughly $36,000 over 15 years excluding maintenance. High-efficiency heat pump with $14,000 first cost after incentives and $1,300 annual heating and cooling utility cost totals roughly $33,500 over the same period. The numbers depend heavily on the rate market but heat pumps come out ahead in most hot-humid climates once operating savings are counted for the whole period.

How do property resale considerations factor in?

Both systems are recognized standards and neither creates a barrier to sale. Buyers in some markets increasingly prefer heat pumps for their electrification story and single-fuel simplicity. Buyers in cold climates and gas-abundant regions often prefer the peak-hour reassurance of a gas furnace. The resale impact is not usually a first-order variable but is worth considering on homes owned for shorter horizons.
